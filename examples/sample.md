# How does a heat pump work?

Heat pumps now heat a large share of new homes in Europe. Still, many people do not know what actually happens inside the device. This text explains the principle, the main types, typical numbers and the limits of the technology.

## The basic idea: move heat instead of making it

An oil or gas boiler burns fuel and turns it into heat. A heat pump does something different: it makes very little heat itself. Instead, it collects heat from the surroundings and lifts it to a higher temperature. The heat comes from the outside air, from the ground or from groundwater. Even air at minus ten degrees Celsius still contains usable energy.

Everybody knows the principle from the fridge. A fridge pulls heat out of its inside and releases it through the black grid at the back into the kitchen. A heat pump does exactly the same – except that the outdoors is the "inside" that gets cooled, and the house is the "kitchen" that gets warm.

## The cycle in four steps

Inside the heat pump, a refrigerant circulates. This is a fluid that boils at very low temperatures. The cycle has four stations:

1. **Evaporate:** In the evaporator, the cold liquid refrigerant takes up heat from the surroundings. Because it boils at such a low temperature, it turns into vapour – even when the outside air is cold.
2. **Compress:** An electric compressor squeezes the vapour. The higher pressure makes its temperature rise sharply, for example from zero to more than sixty degrees.
3. **Condense:** In the condenser, the hot vapour hands its heat over to the water of the heating system. It cools down and turns liquid again.
4. **Expand:** An expansion valve lowers the pressure. This makes the refrigerant very cold again, so it can pick up heat from the surroundings once more.

This cycle runs continuously as long as the house needs heat. The only part that uses a lot of electricity is the compressor.

## The key number: seasonal performance factor

How efficiently a heat pump works is described by the seasonal performance factor, or SPF. It tells you how many kilowatt-hours of heat the system delivers per kilowatt-hour of electricity over a whole year. An SPF of 3 means: one kilowatt-hour of electricity becomes three kilowatt-hours of heat. Two thirds of the energy come for free from the surroundings.

Typical values look like this:

| Type | Heat source | Typical SPF |
|---|---|---|
| Air-to-water heat pump | Outside air | 3.0 |
| Ground-source heat pump | Borehole | 4.2 |
| Water-to-water heat pump | Groundwater | 4.8 |

An example: a single-family house needs 15,000 kilowatt-hours of heat per year. With a ground-source heat pump and an SPF of 4.2, this takes about 3,600 kilowatt-hours of electricity. With an air-to-water heat pump and an SPF of 3.0, it takes about 5,000 kilowatt-hours. An oil boiler would need roughly 1,700 litres of heating oil for the same heat.

## Three types compared

**Air-to-water heat pumps** take heat from the outside air. They are the cheapest to buy and need no drilling. On the other hand, they work worst exactly when it is coldest, and the fan makes noise. In dense neighbourhoods, location and noise protection need care.

**Ground-source heat pumps** use a borehole. A hole 100 to 250 metres deep is drilled into the ground. At that depth, the ground stays at about 10 to 15 degrees all year. So the system works efficiently and quietly even in winter. The drilling, however, is expensive and needs a permit.

**Water-to-water heat pumps** use groundwater. They are the most efficient, but they only work where there is enough groundwater and the authorities allow using it.

## What a heat pump needs

A heat pump works better the smaller the temperature gap between the heat source and the heating system. This leads to a few rules:

- **Low flow temperature:** Underfloor heating or large radiators that work with 35 degrees are ideal. Old, small radiators often need 60 degrees or more.
- **Good insulation:** A well-insulated house needs less heat and lower temperatures.
- **The right size:** A system that is too large keeps switching on and off. That costs efficiency and shortens its life.
- **Proper settings:** Many systems run with poor settings after installation. Tuning them after the first winter pays off.

## Everyday examples

In **new buildings**, a heat pump is almost the default today. The house is well insulated, has underfloor heating and often solar panels on the roof that supply part of the electricity.

In **older buildings**, replacing an oil boiler is possible too. Often it is enough to install a few larger radiators or to insulate the roof. Replacing an oil boiler saves CO₂ and also makes you independent of oil prices.

In **apartment blocks** and whole districts, large heat pumps are often combined with lake water, sewage water or waste heat from data centres. This creates heating networks for entire streets.

Many heat pumps can also **cool** in summer. With a borehole this is especially elegant: the cool ground absorbs heat from the house. This is called "free cooling", because it needs hardly any electricity.

## Limits and risks

A heat pump is not a miracle cure. Important limitations:

- **Electricity demand in winter:** On cold days, heat pumps need a lot of electricity – exactly when solar power is scarce. This puts pressure on the grid.
- **High upfront cost:** A ground-source system for a single-family house can easily cost 40,000 to 60,000 euros. Subsidy programmes cover part of it.
- **Refrigerants:** Some refrigerants are strong greenhouse gases if they leak. Newer systems therefore use natural refrigerants such as propane.
- **Poorly insulated houses:** Without renovation and with high flow temperatures, the SPF drops clearly. Then the heat pump becomes expensive to run.

## Conclusion

A heat pump moves heat from outside to inside – with the same principle as a fridge. One kilowatt-hour of electricity becomes three to five kilowatt-hours of heat. It works best in well-insulated houses with low flow temperatures. Anyone replacing an old oil or gas boiler should therefore not only choose a device, but look at the whole house: insulation, radiators and the source of the electricity all belong together.
